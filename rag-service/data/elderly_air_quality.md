# Older Adults and Air Quality

## Why older adults are at elevated risk
Adults over 65 face higher risk from air pollution because of accumulated lifetime exposure, declining cardiopulmonary reserve, higher prevalence of underlying conditions (coronary artery disease, hypertension, COPD, diabetes), reduced mucociliary clearance, and a higher likelihood of polypharmacy. The same PM2.5 spike that produces no symptoms in a healthy 30-year-old can precipitate a heart failure exacerbation, atrial fibrillation, or ischemic stroke in an 80-year-old with multiple comorbidities.

Studies of acute mortality during smoke events and pollution spikes consistently find the largest absolute increases in deaths among adults over 75.

## Most relevant pollutants
- **PM2.5** is the dominant driver of cardiovascular and respiratory mortality in older adults. Long-term exposure accelerates cognitive decline (linked to higher dementia incidence).
- **Ozone** is associated with respiratory mortality in the elderly during summer episodes.
- **PM10** aggravates COPD and is most relevant during dust storms or near construction.
- **NO2** worsens existing COPD and cardiovascular disease.

## Recommendations by AQI level

**AQI ≤ 50:** Normal activity. Outdoor walking is encouraged; physical activity outweighs pollution risks at this level.

**AQI 51–100:** Continue normal activity. Adults with severe COPD (GOLD III–IV), recent MI, or heart failure exacerbation within 30 days should consider scaling back intense exertion.

**AQI 101–150 (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups):**
- Reduce prolonged outdoor exertion. Walking is fine; gardening or yard work for more than 30–60 minutes is not.
- Avoid arterial roads. Walk in parks or quiet residential streets.
- Monitor blood pressure and pulse oximetry if you have a baseline of doing so. Pollution-driven BP rise is real and additive to existing hypertension.
- Maintain medication compliance — beta blockers, ACE inhibitors, statins, inhalers.

**AQI 151–200 (Unhealthy):**
- Move walks and exercise indoors. A mall or community center walk is a good substitute.
- Run a HEPA filter in the main living area and bedroom.
- N95/FFP2 mask for unavoidable outdoor time when PM2.5 dominates. Verify the mask seals properly; facial hair and ill-fitting masks substantially reduce effectiveness.
- Watch for new or worsening dyspnea, chest tightness, ankle swelling, palpitations, or persistent cough.

**AQI 201–300 (Very Unhealthy):**
- Stay indoors. Run HEPA filtration continuously.
- Avoid all outdoor exertion.
- Adults with COPD or heart failure should have a low threshold to contact their primary care physician about whether to step up bronchodilator or diuretic therapy proactively.

**AQI 301+ (Hazardous):**
- Indoor confinement with HEPA filtration.
- Older adults living alone should arrange daily check-ins from family or caregivers — symptoms of cardiac or respiratory decompensation can develop slowly and be missed.
- Seek emergency care for new dyspnea at rest, chest pain, syncope, confusion, productive cough with fever, or oxygen saturation below 90% on a home pulse oximeter.

## Practical measures for older adults
- HEPA filtration in the bedroom is one of the highest-yield interventions; it improves overnight oxygen saturation and reduces inflammatory markers.
- Avoid using gas stoves without ventilation; indoor NO2 from cooking can rival outdoor concentrations on bad-air days.
- Maintain a current medication list and asthma/COPD action plan accessible to family members.
- Keep at least one week of essential medications on hand to avoid pharmacy trips during multi-day pollution episodes.
- Stay hydrated; dehydration thickens respiratory secretions and worsens exertional dyspnea.
- Continue moderate physical activity overall — cardiopulmonary fitness is protective against pollution-related events on subsequent good-air days.
