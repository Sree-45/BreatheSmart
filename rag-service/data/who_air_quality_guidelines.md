# WHO Global Air Quality Guidelines (2021)

## Overview
The World Health Organization's 2021 Global Air Quality Guidelines tightened recommended pollutant limits in response to mounting evidence that even low concentrations of common air pollutants cause significant health harm. The guidelines apply globally and target six classical pollutants. Health risks from particulate matter and ozone are well established; long-term exposure to PM2.5 is associated with stroke, heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, and acute lower respiratory infections in children.

## Recommended annual mean concentrations
- **PM2.5 (fine particulate matter):** 5 micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m³)
- **PM10 (coarse particulate matter):** 15 µg/m³
- **NO2 (nitrogen dioxide):** 10 µg/m³
- **SO2 (sulfur dioxide):** 40 µg/m³ (24-hour mean)
- **O3 (ozone):** 60 µg/m³ (peak season 8-hour mean)
- **CO (carbon monoxide):** 4 mg/m³ (24-hour mean)

## Recommended 24-hour mean concentrations
- PM2.5: 15 µg/m³ (99th percentile, 3–4 days/year)
- PM10: 45 µg/m³
- NO2: 25 µg/m³
- O3: 100 µg/m³ (8-hour mean)

## Health implications by pollutant
**PM2.5** penetrates deep into the lungs and crosses into the bloodstream. It is the strongest predictor of mortality among regulated pollutants. Short-term spikes increase hospital admissions for cardiovascular and respiratory causes within 1–2 days. People with asthma, COPD, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, or pregnancy are at elevated risk.

**PM10** is dominated by coarser dust and can aggravate the upper airways and trigger asthma attacks. Its effects are most acute during dust storms or near construction.

**NO2** is a marker of traffic-related pollution. Chronic exposure reduces lung development in children and increases the incidence of asthma. Short-term exposure aggravates existing asthma and COPD.

**O3 (ground-level ozone)** is highly reactive and irritates the airways. It peaks on hot, sunny afternoons and is the most relevant pollutant for outdoor workers and athletes during summer. It causes coughing, throat irritation, and reduced lung function on exposure.

**SO2** primarily comes from coal combustion and metal smelting. It induces bronchoconstriction in asthmatics within minutes of exposure to elevated concentrations.

**CO** displaces oxygen on hemoglobin and is most dangerous in enclosed spaces (faulty heaters, vehicle exhaust). At outdoor ambient levels in well-monitored cities CO is rarely the dominant risk.

## Sensitive populations
Children, the elderly (>65), pregnant women, and people with pre-existing respiratory or cardiovascular disease experience adverse effects at lower concentrations than the general population. Outdoor workers and athletes are at elevated risk on high-pollution days because of higher ventilation rates.

## General actions when concentrations are elevated
- Reduce outdoor exertion when PM2.5 exceeds 35 µg/m³ (24-hour) or AQI exceeds 100.
- Close windows and run HEPA-grade filtration indoors during smoke events or industrial spikes.
- N95/FFP2 masks substantially reduce inhaled PM2.5; surgical and cloth masks do not.
- Ozone peaks late afternoon; schedule outdoor activity in the early morning during summer.
- Maintain medication compliance; people with asthma should keep rescue inhalers accessible.
