# Cardiovascular Disease and Air Quality

## The link between PM2.5 and heart disease
Long-term PM2.5 exposure is one of the largest modifiable cardiovascular risk factors at the population level. PM2.5 particles are small enough to cross from lung alveoli into the bloodstream, where they trigger systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Chronic exposure accelerates atherosclerosis, increases blood pressure, promotes thrombosis, and is associated with myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and arrhythmias.

The American Heart Association concludes that there is a causal relationship between PM2.5 exposure and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Each 10 µg/m³ increase in long-term PM2.5 is associated with roughly an 8–18% increase in cardiovascular mortality.

## Acute risks during pollution spikes
Short-term spikes (a single high-pollution day) elevate the risk of acute coronary events within 24–72 hours, particularly in people with established coronary artery disease. Triggers documented in the literature include:
- Increased risk of myocardial infarction (heart attack) on high-PM2.5 days, especially among people over 65.
- Triggering of arrhythmias in patients with implantable defibrillators.
- Worsening of heart failure symptoms within a day of exposure.
- Higher stroke incidence — both ischemic and hemorrhagic — in the 24 hours following spikes.

## At-risk populations
- Adults with established coronary artery disease, heart failure, or prior stroke.
- Adults over 65 (cardiovascular reserve declines with age).
- People with hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome (additive inflammation burden).
- Pregnant women (PM2.5 exposure increases preeclampsia and pre-term birth risk).

## Recommendations by AQI level for people with cardiovascular disease

**AQI ≤ 50:** Normal activity. Maintain medication compliance; aerobic exercise outdoors is beneficial.

**AQI 51–100:** Continue normal activity. People with severe symptoms (NYHA III–IV heart failure, recent MI within 6 weeks) may consider scaling back intense exertion.

**AQI 101–150 (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups):**
- Reduce prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion. Walking and light activity remain fine.
- Avoid outdoor exercise within a few hundred metres of major roadways.
- Monitor blood pressure if on antihypertensives — air pollution can raise BP measurably.
- Anyone with recent cardiac stenting, MI, or heart failure exacerbation should treat this as Unhealthy and limit outdoor exertion.

**AQI 151–200 (Unhealthy):**
- Avoid prolonged outdoor exertion. Move workouts indoors.
- N95/FFP2 mask for unavoidable outdoor time when PM2.5 dominates.
- Maintain medication compliance — do not skip beta blockers, statins, or antihypertensives on bad-air days.
- Watch for warning symptoms: chest pain, dyspnea on minimal exertion, palpitations, lightheadedness.

**AQI 201–300 (Very Unhealthy):**
- Stay indoors with HEPA filtration running.
- Postpone all outdoor exercise.
- Patients with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators should have a low threshold to contact their cardiology team if they receive any shocks.

**AQI 301+ (Hazardous):**
- Indoor confinement with HEPA filtration is the priority.
- Avoid all physical exertion, even light. Increased heart rate increases inhaled dose.
- Seek emergency care for chest pain lasting more than a few minutes, new dyspnea at rest, syncope, or sustained palpitations.

## General lifestyle measures for CV patients
- Run HEPA filtration in bedroom and main living area year-round if you live in an area with annual PM2.5 above 10 µg/m³.
- Avoid running, cycling, and walking along arterial roads. Choose parks and side streets with tree cover when possible.
- N95/FFP2 masks during high-PM2.5 commutes reduce systemic inflammatory markers measurably.
- Maintain blood pressure control; pollution-driven BP increases are additive to baseline hypertension.
- Sustain physical activity overall — moderate exercise outdoors on clean-air days produces cardiovascular benefits that exceed pollution-related risks at most ambient concentrations seen in well-monitored cities.
