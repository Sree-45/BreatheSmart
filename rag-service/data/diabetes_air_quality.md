# Diabetes and Air Quality

## Why people with diabetes are at elevated risk
Diabetes amplifies the cardiovascular harm of air pollution. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) penetrates deep into the airways and crosses into the bloodstream, where it drives systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. People with diabetes show stronger oxidative-stress responses to PM2.5 than people without, and pollution exposure adds to an already-elevated baseline risk of heart attack, arrhythmia, heart failure, and stroke. Exposure to higher PM2.5 is also associated with increases in blood glucose and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) across the glycemic spectrum, and a meaningful share of type 2 diabetes burden globally is linked to chronic PM2.5 exposure. There is no safe lower threshold — effects are seen even at low concentrations.

## What air pollution does in diabetes
- **Acute cardiovascular events:** short-term PM2.5 spikes (hours to weeks) can trigger heart attacks, arrhythmias, and worsening heart failure — risk is higher in people with diabetes.
- **Glucose and blood pressure:** higher PM2.5 is associated with raised blood glucose, HbA1c, and blood pressure.
- **Compounding risk factors:** diabetes often coexists with hypertension, high cholesterol, obesity, and coronary disease, each of which independently raises pollution-related cardiovascular risk.

## Recommendations by AQI level
**AQI ≤ 50 (Good):** Normal activity. A good day for outdoor exercise, which also helps glycemic control.

**AQI 51-100 (Moderate):** Generally fine. If you have established heart disease or neuropathy, prefer low-traffic routes and monitor how you feel during exertion.

**AQI 101-150 (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups):**
- Reduce prolonged or heavy outdoor exertion; take more breaks.
- Avoid arterial roads and rush-hour traffic where PM2.5 and NO2 concentrate.
- Keep taking prescribed medication on schedule; check blood pressure and glucose if you feel unwell.
- Run indoor HEPA filtration and keep windows closed during spikes.

**AQI 151-200 (Unhealthy):**
- Avoid prolonged outdoor exertion; move workouts indoors to filtered air.
- Wear a well-fitted N95/FFP2 for unavoidable outdoor time when PM2.5 dominates.
- Watch for cardiac warning signs: chest tightness, palpitations, unusual fatigue, lightheadedness, or breathlessness.

**AQI 201-300 (Very Unhealthy):**
- Stay indoors with windows closed and a HEPA air cleaner running.
- Avoid all outdoor exertion.
- Monitor blood pressure and glucose; contact your physician if respiratory or cardiovascular symptoms appear, since diabetes amplifies pollution-related risk.

**AQI 301+ (Hazardous):**
- Remain in HEPA-filtered air and keep activity low.
- N95 only for unavoidable outdoor exposure (e.g., evacuation).
- Seek emergency care for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, or symptoms of a cardiac event.

## Daily best practices for diabetes + air pollution
- Check the AQI (e.g., AirNow) and sign up for local pollution/wildfire alerts; plan activity for cleaner hours.
- Run a HEPA air purifier in the bedroom and keep the home smoke-free.
- Maintain medication and monitoring routines (glucose, blood pressure) — pollution days are not the time to skip them.
- A sealed N95/FFP2 substantially reduces inhaled PM2.5; surgical and cloth masks do not.
- Avoid busy roads and idling traffic; ventilate via quieter streets where possible.
